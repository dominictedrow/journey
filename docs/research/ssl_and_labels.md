## 1. MAE (He et al. 2021) — mechanics and why it's cheap

**Masking ratio.** MAE masks a very high proportion of input patches — the paper finds 75% masking "yields a nontrivial and meaningful self-supervisory task," and this ratio is what makes the pretext task non-trivial (low masking makes reconstruction solvable by simple interpolation). Source: [Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners, arXiv:2111.06377](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.06377).

**Encoder/decoder asymmetry.** The encoder runs only on the visible (~25%) patches — no mask tokens enter the encoder at all; the lightweight decoder (a small set of Transformer blocks) takes the encoded visible tokens plus learned mask tokens and reconstructs the missing pixels. Because the encoder never processes masked patches, per-image compute drops roughly in proportion to the visible fraction, which is the core reason MAE pretraining is cheap. Same source.

**Loss.** Reconstruction target is normalized per-patch pixel values (pixel loss computed after per-patch mean/std normalization), and the paper reports this normalization "improves representation quality" over raw-pixel MSE. Same source.

**Speed/scale claims.** The asymmetric design plus high masking ratio lets MAE "accelerate training (by 3x or more)" and scale to large models; ViT-Huge fine-tuned after MAE pretraining reaches 87.8% top-1 on ImageNet-1K among ImageNet-1K-only methods, and MAE pretraining transfers better than supervised pretraining on several downstream tasks. Same source. Exact reference schedule (1600 epochs, batch size, TPU/GPU count) is described in the paper's implementation section but was not independently re-extracted here beyond the epoch count of 1600 seen in secondary sources — treat the exact hardware/batch numbers as `UNVERIFIED` pending direct read of the paper's appendix.

## 2. VideoMAE (Tong et al. 2022) — data efficiency, exact claims

Source: [VideoMAE: Masked Autoencoders are Data-Efficient Learners for Self-Supervised Video Pre-Training, arXiv:2203.12602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12602).

- **Tube masking:** masks the *same* spatial locations across all sampled frames of a clip (rather than independently per frame), which prevents the model from "cheating" via trivial temporal interpolation across adjacent unmasked frames — directly analogous to masking the same in-plane region across adjacent MRI slices.
- **Masking ratio:** 90–95% (far higher than 2D-MAE's 75%), justified because video/volumetric content is more temporally/spatially redundant than a single 2D image — the same redundancy argument applies to adjacent MRI slices within a series.
- **Data efficiency headline claim:** "VideoMAE achieves impressive results on very small datasets (i.e., around 3k–4k videos) without using any extra data" — i.e., it does not need ImageNet-scale or Kinetics-scale pretraining data to produce useful representations, which is the single most load-bearing citation for this project (we have on the order of thousands of studies, not millions of images).
- **Reported downstream accuracies (full pretraining, not the small-dataset ablation):** 87.4% Kinetics-400, 75.4% Something-Something V2, 91.3% UCF101, 62.6% HMDB51.
- **Efficiency:** pretraining is reported as up to 3.2x faster than contrastive alternatives owing to the high masking ratio reducing per-iteration compute.
- **Key stated takeaway:** "data quality is more important than data quantity for SSVP [self-supervised video pre-training]" — relevant since our unlabeled pool (~7,500 studies) is small relative to natural-video SSL corpora but each study contains dozens of correlated slices, i.e., the same "redundant, low-quantity-but-high-signal" regime VideoMAE targets.

**Implication for this project:** treating a knee-MRI series (20–45 slices, per CLAUDE.md) as a "clip" and masking spatiotemporal tubes across the slice axis is a defensible, literature-backed way to get useful self-supervised signal even though we only have thousands, not millions, of studies — provided the effective number of *distinct spatial patches seen* is large (each series contributes many masked reconstruction problems per epoch, not just one).

## 3. MAE for medical imaging / MRI / 3D

**Self Pre-training with Masked Autoencoders (SelfMAE), Zhou et al. 2022**, [arXiv:2203.05573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05573), presented at ISBI 2023. Core idea directly applicable here: because there is no ImageNet-scale *medical* pretraining corpus, pretrain the MAE directly on the target dataset's own (unlabeled) training images rather than transferring from natural-image MAE weights ("self pre-training"). Reported to "markedly improve" chest X-ray classification, abdominal CT multi-organ segmentation, and MRI brain tumor segmentation versus baselines. Exact masking ratio, epoch count, and per-task AUC/Dice deltas were not extractable from the abstract/metadata alone via automated fetch in this session — code is public at `cvlab-stonybrook/SelfMedMAE`; treat specific ablation numbers as `UNVERIFIED` until the PDF is read directly.

**3D MAE compute references on consumer/single-GPU hardware** (useful as compute-budget anchors, from a broader search of recent 3D-medical-MAE papers):
- A multi-scale-fusion 3D CT MAE ran pretraining on a **single GPU, batch size 2, 100 epochs**. [Multi-Scale Fusion Masked Autoencoder, ACM ICAICI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3730436.3730512).
- BM-MAE (multimodal brain-MRI MAE) pretrained **1000 epochs, batch size 6, on a single RTX 4090**. [arXiv:2505.00568](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00568v1) — also reports BM-MAE gives an average **+16.9% AUC** (regular variant **+19.7% AUC**) over random-init training on downstream brain-tumor tasks — the single largest quantified MAE-vs-scratch AUC delta found in this search, useful as an optimistic upper bound for "how much does MAE pretraining buy you over training from scratch" in an MRI setting. Same source.
- Intensity-Spatial Dual MAE for chest CT: **50 epochs on a single RTX 4070 Ti Super**. [arXiv:2411.13198](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.13198).
- Reported epoch counts for 3D medical MAE variants in the literature range very widely (50–10,000 epochs depending on dataset size and architecture) — there is no single "standard" schedule; smaller datasets tend to use more epochs at smaller batch size on a single consumer GPU. Treat any specific epoch count as dataset-specific, not a rule.

**3DINO (3D self-distillation with no labels v2)** — not MAE, but the most directly comparable large 3D-medical-SSL system found, so noted here for calibration: adapts DINOv2 to 3D medical volumes, pretrained on **~100,000 3D scans** (MRI N=70,434; CT N=27,815; brain PET N=566) across 10+ organs, reported to outperform prior pretrained 3D medical models on multiple downstream tasks. [A generalizable 3D framework and model for self-supervised learning in medical imaging, npj Digital Medicine / arXiv:2501.11755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.11755); [GitHub](https://github.com/AICONSlab/3DINO). This is ~13–100x more volumes than our ~7,500-study pool, and was trained at an institutional-cluster scale (not single-GPU) — it is a ceiling reference for what "real" 3D medical SSL foundation models look like, not something reproducible on the project's RTX 5070.

**Knee-MRI-specific SSL precedent:**
- **SKID** (Self-supervised learning for Knee Injury Diagnosis), [arXiv:2104.10481](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.10481): applies SSL to knee MR "video" frames for class-imbalanced multi-label diagnosis; claimed to be first SSL work on this imbalanced MR-video task. - **SB-SSL** (slice-based self-supervised transformers) reportedly reached **89.17% accuracy / 0.954 AUC** for ACL tear identification with under 1,000 cases, without external pretraining data, per search-engine synthesis of [arXiv:2208.13923](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.13923) — treat this AUC as `UNVERIFIED` until confirmed by reading the paper directly (it came from an aggregated search summary, not a direct primary-source read).

## 4. DINO / DINOv2 / iBOT / SimCLR / MoCo v3 in medical 3D — comparison to MAE

- **Contrastive vs MAE head-to-head on small/imbalanced medical data:** [Self-Supervised Pre-Training with Contrastive and Masked Autoencoder Methods for Dealing with Small Datasets in Deep Learning for Medical Imaging, arXiv:2308.06534](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.06534) directly compares MoCo, SwAV, BYOL (contrastive family) against SparK (a convolutional masked-autoencoder-style method) pretrained on 244,527 unlabeled CT slices, fine-tuned on downstream sets ranging 145–13,952 images. **Finding: all pretraining methods beat no pretraining, and SparK (MAE-family) is more robust than the contrastive methods as fine-tuning dataset size shrinks** — the paper explicitly recommends the MAE-style approach "for medical imaging tasks with only small annotated datasets." This is a fairly direct, primary-source answer to "which SSL family wins when labeled data is scarce" and favors this project's implicit MAE-first instinct. Exact numeric AUC/accuracy deltas were not extractable from the abstract alone in this session.
- **DINOv2 adapted to 3D via 2D-slice aggregation:** Medical Slice Transformer (MST) extends 2D DINOv2 features to 3D medical volumes by aggregating per-slice DINOv2 embeddings with a Transformer, reported to improve diagnostic accuracy and explainability over CNN baselines. [arXiv:2411.15802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15802) / [Scientific Reports version](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-09041-8). This is a lower-engineering-effort alternative to training a 3D encoder from scratch: use an off-the-shelf pretrained 2D DINOv2 (trained on natural images, publicly available, allowed under the competition's "freely available pretrained models" rule) as a frozen or lightly fine-tuned per-slice feature extractor, then learn a lightweight slice-aggregation Transformer on top — but this competes with, rather than validates, the "ViT from scratch" learning goal Dom set for this project, so it should be considered only as a fallback benchmark, not the primary path.
- **3DINO vs MAE-style methods on MRI:** one source note states "MAE with spectral-domain supervision consistently achieves superior downstream performance for MRI-based disease detection" versus DINO-style self-distillation, though this appeared only inside aggregated search synthesis, not a directly re-read primary source in this session — `UNVERIFIED`, flagged for follow-up if a head-to-head MAE-vs-DINOv2 MRI benchmark decision is needed.
- **Practicality on a single 12 GB GPU:** MoCo-style methods use a memory/queue mechanism so they don't need SimCLR's large in-batch negative-sample batch sizes, making them more batch-size-friendly for constrained VRAM; MAE-family methods are inherently the cheapest of all these options per training step because the encoder only ever sees the unmasked ~10–25% of tokens (see §1–2). Given the 12 GB VRAM / 16 GB RAM constraint noted in CLAUDE.md, **MAE-style pretraining is the more practical single-GPU choice both for memory (no large-batch contrastive negatives needed) and for literature-backed effectiveness on small/imbalanced medical data** (§3, §4 first bullet).

## 5. Recommended SSL recipe for this project

This section is `[derived]` — a synthesis/recommendation built from §1–4, not a direct quote from any single source; cross-check against your own pilot runs.

**What to pretrain on:** 2D-slice MAE first, not full 3D-volume MAE, for a first pass. Rationale: (a) every 3D-medical-MAE precedent found above that ran on a single consumer GPU used small batch sizes (2–6) even for 3D CT/MRI (§3), which will be a hard fit alongside a from-scratch ViT training loop on a 12 GB card; (b) 2D-slice MAE lets every slice in every series act as an independent (though correlated) training example, multiplying effective sample count — ~7,500 studies × ~30 median slices (CLAUDE.md) ≈ 225,000 slice images, a much friendlier number for MAE than 7,500 volumes; (c) VideoMAE's "tube masking across correlated frames" (§2) can then be layered on as a second stage once the 2D encoder is validated, by masking the same spatial tube across a short stack of adjacent slices (treating slice-order as the temporal axis, consistent with CLAUDE.md's own framing of "temporal/slice-dropout"). This staged 2D→2.5D/3D plan lets you validate cheaply before committing to the more expensive volumetric pretraining.

**Masking ratio:** start at MAE's canonical 75% for the 2D-slice stage (§1); if/when moving to slice-tube masking, push toward VideoMAE's 90–95% (§2), since adjacent MRI slices are at least as redundant as adjacent video frames, arguably more so within a single series/sequence.

**Epoch / time budget on a single consumer GPU (RTX 5070, ~12 GB):** no single "correct" number exists in the literature (§3 epoch range 50–10,000 depending on setup), but the closest useful anchor is ["MAE are Secretly Efficient Learners"](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024W/ECV24/papers/Wei_Masked_Autoencoders_are_Secretly_Efficient_Learners__CVPRW_2024_paper.pdf) ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=dGRP5SfwkgY)), which reports pretraining a **ViT-B in 12.6 hours on a single A100 GPU**, reaching 83.0% top-1 on downstream ImageNet classification — i.e., a full, competitive MAE pretraining run is achievable in roughly half a day on one modern datacenter GPU. The RTX 5070 is a consumer card with less compute and memory bandwidth than an A100, so treat 12.6 hours as an optimistic floor; budget on the order of **1–3 days of wall-clock time** for a ViT-S/ViT-B-scale 2D-slice MAE pretraining run on ~225k slice images at a smaller batch size than A100 setups would use — this is a `[derived]` estimate, not a sourced number, and should be corrected after a short timed pilot (e.g., time 1 epoch, extrapolate).

**Fine-tuning after pretraining:** use **layer-wise learning-rate decay (LLRD)**, standard practice in the MAE fine-tuning recipe and shown to matter: a closely related efficiency paper reports LLRD contributing roughly a **+2% top-1 accuracy** improvement over the original MAE fine-tuning recipe without it, with typical decay factors around **0.75 for ViT-S/16** and **0.65 for ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/16** (earlier/lower layers get progressively smaller learning rates than the head). [arXiv:2303.13496, "The effectiveness of MAE pre-pretraining for billion-scale pretraining"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.13496); LLRD discussion also appears in the broader MAE fine-tuning literature summarized via [arXiv search synthesis](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=dGRP5SfwkgY) — treat the exact decay-factor numbers as reasonable defaults to start from, not settled for this specific dataset.

**Bottom line recommendation:** Pretrain a 2D-slice MAE (ViT-S or ViT-B scale, 75% masking, AdamW, cosine schedule) directly on the project's own ~225k unlabeled slice images ("self pre-training," per Zhou et al., §3) rather than relying solely on natural-image-pretrained weights, since medical MAE self-pretraining has repeatedly been shown to beat both scratch and ImageNet-supervised transfer at these data scales (§3–4). Fine-tune with LLRD once pseudo-labels or report-derived labels are available (§6–7 below). Treat volumetric/tube MAE and DINOv2-style alternatives as second-stage experiments, not the first commitment, given the single-GPU memory/time constraints.

## 6. Extracting structured findings from radiology reports

### 6.1 Rule-based labelers: CheXpert labeler, NegBio

- **NegBio** is a rule-based negation/uncertainty detector for radiology reports; **CheXpert's labeler improves on NegBio** with "more controlled extraction of mentions and an improved NLP pipeline and rule set for uncertainty and negation extraction," and is reported to significantly outperform NegBio across chest-X-ray observations on mention extraction, negation, and uncertainty detection. Source: search synthesis referencing the original [CheXpert paper, arXiv:1901.07031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07031) and [CheXbert paper, arXiv:2004.09167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09167) — the specific comparative numbers below come from the CheXbert paper's own reported evaluation of the CheXpert labeler.
- **CheXpert labeler accuracy (from the CheXbert evaluation):** on 1,000 held-out reports, Mention F1 **0.948**, Negation F1 **0.899**, Uncertainty F1 **0.770**. On an out-of-distribution set of 687 MIMIC-CXR reports, performance dropped substantially: **0.874 / 0.565 / 0.470** respectively for the same three tasks. Source: [CheXbert, arXiv:2004.09167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09167).
  - **This out-of-distribution drop is the single most important cautionary data point for this project**: a rule-based labeler tuned on one institution's reporting style can lose 30+ F1 points on negation/uncertainty when applied to a different reporting style. RSNA knee reports come from **16+ sites in multiple languages** (per project CLAUDE.md), so a rule set built for one language/site is very unlikely to transfer cleanly — this favors either (a) a multilingual LLM-based approach (§6.3) or (b) building/validating rules per-language/per-site against the 58 verified studies before trusting them at scale.
- **German CheXpert-style labeler** exists as a precedent for non-English rule-based/DL labelers ([arXiv:2306.02777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.02777), [arXiv:2306.05997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05997)), confirming that porting CheXpert-style rule sets to other languages is a known, done-before (but nontrivial, requires re-engineering) exercise — relevant if the knee report corpus is dominated by one or two non-English languages.

### 6.2 BERT-based labeler: CheXbert

CheXbert replaces CheXpert's hand-written rules with a BERT model first trained on CheXpert-labeler outputs, then fine-tuned on a small set of expert annotations augmented via backtranslation. Reported result: **F1 0.798 vs CheXpert's 0.743** (statistically significant, p<0.001), with improvement in all but 2 of 14 conditions and significant improvement in 9/14; also **~140x faster at inference** (<12 seconds to label 1,000 reports vs 28 minutes for the rule-based labeler). Source: [CheXbert, arXiv:2004.09167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09167).

**Relevance:** CheXbert's recipe — train a rule-based labeler first, distill it into a small BERT model, then fine-tune the BERT model on a small set of *expert-verified* labels — is structurally very close to this project's own labeling plan (fine-tune a labeler on the 58 verified studies). CheXbert's "small set of expert annotations" for its final fine-tuning step was on the order of a few hundred reports in the original chest X-ray setting (exact count not re-verified in this session, `UNVERIFIED`) — worth confirming, since it bears directly on whether 58 verified studies is enough for an analogous fine-tuning step here.

### 6.3 LLM-based report labeling (GPT-4, open models, multilingual)

- **GPT-4o zero-shot structured extraction from musculoskeletal radiography reports:** reported **>98% accuracy** extracting structured labels for clavicle/elbow/thumb radiographs in zero-shot mode, filling templates with present/absent/uncertain values; performance generalized to external sites, with stronger performance on common fracture-related findings than on rarer soft-tissue findings. Source: [Large Language Model-Based Uncertainty-Adjusted Label Extraction for AI Model Development in Upper Extremity Radiography, arXiv:2510.05664](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05664) / [European Radiology version](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00330-025-12102-1).
  - Directly relevant precedent: (a) it's musculoskeletal imaging (not chest), closer to knee MRI than most CheXpert-family work; (b) it explicitly handles a 3-way present/absent/uncertain scheme, matching the "graded/uncertain finding" problem this project faces (§6.4); (c) the caveat that rarer findings perform worse maps onto this project's rarer labels (e.g., Fracture, Baker's cyst, Contusion likely have low prevalence).
- **GPT-4 for multilingual structured-report transformation:** a feasibility study specifically tested GPT-4 converting free-text radiology reports into structured reports across multiple languages. Source: [Leveraging GPT-4 for Post Hoc Transformation of Free-text Radiology Reports into Structured Reporting: A Multilingual Feasibility Study, Radiology (RSNA journal), 2024](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/radiol.230725) — directly on-point given this project's multilingual `Report` field, but exact per-language accuracy numbers were not extracted in this session (page not fetched in full; `UNVERIFIED`, high priority to re-fetch given direct relevance — it is published in an RSNA journal, the same organizer, and titled exactly on-topic).

Source: [Performance of an Open-Source Large Language Model in Extracting Information from Free-Text Radiology Reports, Radiology: AI, 2024](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/ryai.230364) — again another RSNA-journal, directly on-topic paper not yet fully read in this session; exact accuracy numbers `UNVERIFIED`, flagged as a priority follow-up read (it would directly answer "is a local open LLM good enough").

### 6.4 Handling uncertainty and graded findings

- **CheXpert/MIMIC-CXR convention:** each finding gets one of Positive (1.0) / Negative (0.0) / Uncertain (-1.0) / Not-mentioned, with **U-Ones** (map uncertain → positive) and **U-Zeros** (map uncertain → negative) as the two standard binarization policies for training, plus **U-Ignore** (drop uncertain-labeled examples from the loss for that finding) and **U-MultiClass** (keep uncertainty as a third class) as alternatives. Reported CheXpert validation AUCs: **U-Zeros mean AUC 0.889 vs U-Ones mean AUC 0.893** (close, policy choice is a second-order effect on average), but the best-performing single config in the original CheXpert paper combined **U-Ones + label smoothing + a specific conditional-training (CT) architecture, reaching AUC 0.894** — and critically, **the best policy is pathology-dependent**: U-Ones helped Atelectasis specifically while U-MultiClass was best for Cardiomegaly. Sources: [CheXpert, arXiv:1901.07031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07031); [Interpreting chest X-rays via CNNs that exploit hierarchical disease dependencies and uncertainty labels, arXiv:1911.06475](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.06475).
  - **Implication for this project:** don't pick one global uncertain-handling policy; treat it as a per-finding hyperparameter to sweep (at minimum U-Ones vs U-Zeros vs U-Ignore), and expect the winning policy to differ across the 12 RSNA knee findings, exactly as it did across the 14 CheXpert findings.
- **Graded findings (the project's specific problem: OA grade, "small effusion," "mild" vs "moderate" vs "severe"):** the general pattern in the literature is to first extract a fine-grained/ordinal severity label (e.g., none/mild/moderate/severe, coded 0–3, as done for pulmonary edema severity — [arXiv:2008.05975](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.05975)) and then binarize per-task, since the competition's target labels are binary. There is no single accepted threshold for "how small is small enough to count as positive" found in this search — **this is a genuine open design choice that needs a rule Dom (or an LLM prompt) decides explicitly and applies consistently**, e.g. "any explicitly stated effusion, regardless of size qualifier, counts as positive; only truly negated ('no effusion') or unmentioned counts as negative" is one defensible convention, mirroring how CheXpert generally treats any definite positive mention (regardless of severity qualifier) as positive and only explicit negation as negative — but this exact convention was not found stated verbatim for OA/effusion-style findings in this search and should be treated as a project decision, not an established standard. Flag as `[Dom decision needed]`.
- **"Cannot exclude" / "possible" style hedges:** functionally equivalent to CheXpert's "Uncertain" category — treat with the same U-Ones/U-Zeros/U-Ignore sweep rather than inventing new machinery.

## 7. Pseudo-labeling / self-training with a tiny (58-study) labeled seed

### 7.1 Noisy Student (Xie et al. 2020)

Source: [Self-training with Noisy Student improves ImageNet classification, arXiv:1911.04252](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04252). Trains a teacher on labeled data, uses it (un-noised, for maximally accurate pseudo-labels) to label a much larger unlabeled pool, then trains an equal-or-larger student on the combined labeled+pseudo-labeled data *with* noise injected (dropout, stochastic depth, RandAugment) so the student is regularized beyond the teacher; the process can be iterated by promoting the student to teacher. Headline result: **88.4% top-1 on ImageNet**, +2.0 points over a prior SOTA method that used 3.5B weakly-labeled Instagram images, plus large robustness gains (ImageNet-A 61.0%→83.7%, ImageNet-C mCE 45.7→28.3, ImageNet-P mFR 27.8→12.2). Pseudo-labels can be **soft** (full distribution) or **hard** (argmax/one-hot); the paper's design choices (un-noised teacher for labeling, noised student for training) are directly transferable to this project's "58-study teacher → 7,500-study student" plan.

### 7.2 FixMatch, Meta Pseudo Labels, and confirmation bias

- **Confirmation bias** is the core named failure mode: a pseudo-labeling teacher becomes overconfident and reinforces its own mistakes when the student is trained on its pseudo-labels, especially destructive with very few labeled examples. Source: [Pseudo-Labeling and Confirmation Bias in Deep Semi-Supervised Learning, arXiv:1908.02983](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.02983).
- **FixMatch** combats this with a confidence threshold — only pseudo-labels above a fixed confidence cutoff are used, combined with consistency regularization across weak/strong augmentations, rather than using every teacher prediction. With very few labeled examples (search-synthesis example: 500 labeled CIFAR-10 images, batch size 100, only ~1 clean labeled example per batch on average), naive approaches badly underfit the true label distribution; label-oversampling (forcing a minimum count of *真* labeled examples per minibatch) is one documented mitigation. Source: [An analysis of over-sampling labeled data in semi-supervised learning with FixMatch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.00604).
- **Meta Pseudo Labels** goes further: the teacher is updated based on how the student performs on real labeled data (via a meta-learning/bilevel signal), rather than being frozen after its initial training — explicitly designed to reduce confirmation bias by keeping the teacher responsive to the student's actual generalization, rather than letting a static, possibly-overfit teacher dictate labels indefinitely. Source: [Meta Pseudo Labels, arXiv:2003.10580](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.10580).
- **58 examples is an extremely small teacher-training set** relative to every cited precedent — even FixMatch's "very few labeled examples" ablations (hundreds, e.g. 500 for CIFAR-10) are an order of magnitude larger than 58, and CIFAR-10 is a 10-class problem with abundant per-class images, whereas this project has **12 separate binary findings on only 58 studies**, meaning some individual findings may have single-digit positive examples (§8 quantifies this). This strongly suggests naive teacher/student pseudo-labeling here sits well outside the regime where Noisy Student / FixMatch / Meta Pseudo Labels were validated, and confirmation bias risk should be treated as high, not theoretical — consistent with the caution Dom already flagged in CLAUDE.md.

### 7.3 Why a 58-study image teacher is likely much weaker than report-derived labels — quantified

No paper directly benchmarks "58-example image classifier" against "report-derived NLP labels" for this exact task, so this sub-section is **`[derived]`**, combining the statistical estimates in §8 with the labeler-accuracy numbers in §6:

- A rule-based or LLM-based report labeler, even in its *worse-case* out-of-distribution regime (CheXpert labeler's 0.874/0.565/0.470 mention/negation/uncertainty F1 on unseen report style, §6.1), is still being validated against **effectively the entire training corpus** (~7,500 reports) and correcting/adapting is possible by checking disagreements against the 58 verified studies. A teacher CNN/ViT trained on 58 image-only studies, by contrast, has to learn 12 separate visual decision boundaries from a training set where (§8) several labels likely have fewer than 10 positive examples — this is far below the sample sizes used in *any* of the small-medical-data SSL/classification papers surveyed in §3–4 (which used hundreds to tens of thousands of fine-tuning images even in their "small data" regimes, e.g. 145–13,952 images in §4's contrastive-vs-MAE comparison).
- **Conclusion, consistent with Dom's own stated ordering in CLAUDE.md:** the report-derived-label path (§6) should be treated as the primary/higher-confidence label source, and the 58-study image-only labeler as a cross-check / secondary signal rather than the primary pseudo-labeler for the bulk of the unlabeled set — the literature reviewed here does not contain a single precedent for training a usable *from-images-alone* teacher on an n=58 multi-label dataset, whereas report-NLP-based labeling on thousands of reports (even multilingual, even with degraded out-of-distribution accuracy) has multiple direct precedents (§6.1–6.3) at accuracies in the 0.74–0.95+ F1/accuracy range depending on method and finding.

## 8. Learning with noisy labels

- **Label smoothing:** standard, cheap regularizer against label noise; used as part of the best-performing CheXpert configuration alongside U-Ones (§6.4).
- **Co-teaching:** trains two networks simultaneously, each selecting low-loss (presumed-clean) examples to teach the other, based on the idea that mislabeled examples tend to produce higher loss early in training ("small-loss criterion"); a documented drawback is that consistently selecting only "easy," small-loss examples can slow learning on legitimately hard-but-clean examples. Source: search synthesis of recent noisy-label-learning surveys, e.g. [Collaborative Learning of Sample Selection and Robust Functions for Noisy Labels](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-96-6975-2_14).
- **GCE (Generalized Cross-Entropy)** and **SCE (Symmetric Cross-Entropy):** robust loss functions designed to be less sensitive to label noise than standard cross-entropy; GCE can be understood as interpolating between cross-entropy and mean-absolute-error loss (MAE loss is more noise-robust but harder to optimize; GCE gets some of each). SCE adds a "reverse cross-entropy" term to standard CE. Sources: [Generalized Cross Entropy Loss for Training Deep Neural Networks with Noisy Labels, NeurIPS 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3327546.3327555); symmetric cross-entropy discussion via [IEEE, Learning from Noisy Labeled Data Using Symmetric Cross-Entropy Loss](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9291873/).
- **Small-loss selection** as a general family: the working assumption across these methods is that in early training, correctly-labeled examples tend to have smaller loss than mislabeled ones, so filtering/down-weighting high-loss examples (especially early in training) is a cheap, broadly-applicable defense against noisy labels — applicable directly to a report-derived-label training set, where reports/regex extraction will inevitably mislabel some studies.
- **Precedent for report-derived noisy labels specifically:** the CheXpert U-Ones/U-Zeros/U-Ignore/U-MultiClass framework (§6.4) *is* the field's standard way of handling one specific, well-characterized source of report-label noise (linguistic uncertainty), and label smoothing was explicitly part of CheXpert's best config. There is no separate widely-cited "RSNA/CheXpert-style" noisy-label-correction pipeline beyond this uncertainty-policy sweep plus generic label smoothing found in this search — i.e., **the field's answer to "how do you handle report-derived label noise" is largely "choose an uncertain-mention policy per finding + label smoothing," not an exotic noisy-label algorithm** — which is good news for implementation simplicity but means co-teaching/GCE/SCE are supplementary options to try if the simpler approach underperforms, not the established baseline.

## 9. Validation design with only 58 gold-labeled studies

### 9.1 How noisy is macro-AUC on n=58?

Using the **Hanley–McNeil (1982)** asymptotic standard-error formula for AUC:

SE(AUC) = sqrt[ AUC(1-AUC) + (N₁-1)(Q₁-AUC²) + (N₂-1)(Q₂-AUC²) ] / sqrt(N₁·N₂)

with Q₁ = AUC / (2-AUC), Q₂ = 2·AUC² / (1+AUC), N₁ = # positives, N₂ = # negatives. Source for the formula: [Confidence Intervals for the Area Under an ROC Curve, NCSS/PASS documentation](https://www.ncss.com/wp-content/themes/ncss/pdf/Procedures/PASS/Confidence_Intervals_for_the_Area_Under_an_ROC_Curve.pdf), consistent with the original Hanley & McNeil 1982 Radiology paper.

`[derived]` — computed here for scenarios plausible on the project's 58-study verified set (a rare finding with only ~5 positives out of 58, vs. a more balanced 29/29 split), at three illustrative AUC levels:

| Scenario (N₁ pos / N₂ neg) | True AUC | SE(AUC) | Approx. 95% CI (±1.96·SE) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 / 53 (rare finding) | 0.70 | 0.136 | ±0.267 |
| 5 / 53 (rare finding) | 0.80 | 0.122 | ±0.240 |
| 5 / 53 (rare finding) | 0.90 | 0.094 | ±0.184 |
| 29 / 29 (balanced) | 0.70 | 0.069 | ±0.135 |
| 29 / 29 (balanced) | 0.80 | 0.059 | ±0.115 |
| 29 / 29 (balanced) | 0.90 | 0.042 | ±0.083 |

**Reading this table:** for any of the 12 findings where the 58-study verified set has only a handful of positives (plausible for Fracture, Baker's cyst, Contusion per the project's own note that "some labels may have very few positives"), a measured single-label AUC of, say, 0.80 on the held-out verified set carries a 95% CI of roughly **0.56 to 1.00** — i.e., **statistically indistinguishable from chance at the low end**. Even in the best case (a perfectly balanced 29/29 split, which is unlikely to hold for most of the 12 findings simultaneously), the CI half-width is still ±0.08–0.14, which is comparable to or larger than plausible model-vs-model AUC differences that would matter for leaderboard ranking.

**Macro-AUC over 12 findings** will be somewhat less noisy than any single finding's AUC, because averaging reduces variance — `[derived]`, not sourced: if the 12 per-finding AUC estimation errors were fully independent, the macro-average's SE would shrink by roughly 1/√12 (≈0.29x) relative to a typical single-finding SE; but since all 12 findings are measured on the *same* 58 studies (shared sampling noise, correlated findings — e.g. a study with multiple comorbid findings), the true reduction will be smaller than 1/√12. Treat the 58-study macro-AUC as **informative for large, obviously-broken models (e.g., a genuinely random model vs. a genuinely working one) but not precise enough to reliably rank two models with real AUC differences under ~0.05–0.10** on this verified set alone.

### 9.2 Bootstrap CIs

For small, non-Gaussian, or ties-heavy samples (all likely at n=58), a **nonparametric bootstrap** CI is generally recommended over the closed-form Hanley–McNeil/DeLong approach, which can be conservative or inaccurate outside its asymptotic regime; specialized small-sample corrections such as **wild-bootstrap** methods have been developed specifically because standard approaches can lose their nominal coverage in small samples. Source: search synthesis referencing [Confidence interval for the AUC of SROC curve ... using bootstrap for meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy studies, arXiv:2004.04339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04339) and general small-sample AUC-CI guidance. Practical recommendation: bootstrap-resample the 58 studies (with replacement) at least 1,000–2,000 times, recompute macro-AUC each time, and report the 2.5/97.5 percentiles rather than relying on a single point estimate — this is cheap to implement and gives an empirically honest (if still wide) picture of validation noise, consistent with the Hanley–McNeil estimates in §9.1.

### 9.3 Implication: validation should not rely on the 58 studies alone

Given §9.1–9.2, **a held-out slice of the report-derived-label training set (large-n, per §6, even if individually noisier per-label than the 58 gold studies) should be used as a secondary validation signal alongside the 58-study gold set** — a large noisy-label validation set trades per-example label accuracy for enough volume to actually distinguish model quality, which is precisely the opposite failure mode of the 58-study set (few, more-trustworthy examples, but statistically unable to distinguish real model improvements from noise). Comparing model rankings across *both* the tiny-but-clean set and the large-but-noisy set, and trusting a change only when both agree in direction, is a reasonable practical compromise `[derived]`, and is consistent with the project's own stated plan to cross-check the two labeling paths against each other (CLAUDE.md §Labeling plan).

## Recommended SSL + labeling pipeline for our project

1. **SSL pretraining (unlabeled images, all ~7,500 studies):** 2D-slice MAE (ViT-S or ViT-B, 75% random masking, self-pretrained directly on this project's own slice corpus rather than transferring only from natural-image MAE weights, per §3's "self pre-training" precedent) as the first-stage encoder pretraining. Budget roughly 1–3 days of wall-clock time on the RTX 5070 for a full run (derived estimate, §5 — re-check with a timed 1-epoch pilot before committing). If a second iteration is warranted, extend to slice-tube masking at 90–95% ratio (VideoMAE-style, §2 and §5) across short adjacent-slice stacks, treating slice order as the "temporal" axis already named in CLAUDE.md's augmentation notes.
2. **Fine-tune the pretrained encoder** with layer-wise learning-rate decay (start ~0.65–0.75 depending on model depth, §5) once labels (from step 3 or 4) are available; do not fine-tune the whole network at a uniform LR.
3. **Primary label source — report-derived labels (§6):** build/validate a report labeler in the following order of increasing sophistication, checking each against the 58 verified studies before trusting it at scale: (a) simple rule-based negation/uncertainty extraction (CheXpert-labeler-style, adapted per language) as a cheap first pass; (b) a locally-run open-weights LLM (7–8B, Q4 quantized, fits 12 GB VRAM, §6.5) doing structured present/absent/uncertain extraction per finding, following the GPT-4o present/absent/uncertain template pattern shown effective on musculoskeletal reports (§6.3) — run locally rather than via API until the MIRA license's third-party-API terms are confirmed (open action item, §6.5). Sweep U-Ones vs U-Zeros vs U-Ignore per finding rather than picking one global policy (§6.4), and make an explicit, written convention for graded findings ("small effusion," OA grade) since no external standard settles this (§6.4, flagged `[Dom decision needed]`).
4. **Secondary/cross-check label source — image-only pseudo-labeling from the 58 verified studies (§7):** only after step 3 exists, train a small teacher on the 58 studies (holding out a subset purely for evaluating the teacher, per CLAUDE.md's own mitigation plan) and use it as a *cross-check* against report-derived labels, not as the primary pseudo-labeler for the full unlabeled set — the literature surveyed here has no precedent for a usable from-images-alone teacher at n=58 (§7.3), and confirmation-bias risk (§7.2) is high at this scale. Use soft pseudo-labels + a confidence threshold (FixMatch-style, §7.2) if this path is used at all, and treat any disagreement between the two label sources as a signal to manually inspect that study's report rather than to trust one source blindly.
5. **Noisy-label training:** label smoothing plus a chosen uncertain-mention policy (§6.4/§8) as the default; escalate to GCE/SCE or small-loss-based sample filtering only if validation shows the simple approach underperforming.
6. **Validation:** report both (a) the 58-study gold-label macro-AUC with a bootstrap 95% CI (§9.2, expect it to be wide — a single-digit-positive-count finding can have a CI half-width over 0.2, §9.1), and (b) a large held-out report-derived-label validation slice, and only trust a modeling change that improves (or at least doesn't visibly hurt) both.
